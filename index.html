Why can understanding change before feeling does?

Imagine someone you care about hasn't replied. You feel alarmed. Later, you learn their phone battery died. You believe the explanation—but your alarm may take time to settle.

The project asks three separate questions. What response tends to happen? What response fits the evidence under stated criteria? What response is attainable within a given time? None of these automatically answers the others.

Belief: “The silence has a harmless explanation.”
Feeling: Alarm may still persist.
Reachability: The chance of settling can depend on time, context, and feasible actions.

Here we assume just two possible states: high alarm and low alarm. The person begins in high alarm. At each step there is a fixed chance of moving to low alarm, which then persists. This is deliberately simplified.

How the calculation works: Probability of low alarm after h steps = 1 − (1 − p)h, where p is the chance of settling per step. “Reachable” here means the probability meets the threshold you selected, not that someone can simply choose to feel differently. The model has no measured human parameters, individual differences, setbacks, or clinical validity.

What this demonstrates—and what it doesn't

Even if low alarm is the response preferred by the example's explicitly chosen evaluative rule, it need not be likely within three steps. Change the assumptions and the prediction changes. This is a mathematical illustration of the distinction, not evidence that real people follow this transition rule.

Our empirical work has found predictive information in earlier affect reports, but that does not by itself establish a distinct slow emotional mechanism. The wider research examines measurement, context, history, and competing explanations.
