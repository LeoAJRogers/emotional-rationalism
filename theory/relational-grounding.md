# Relational grounding (theory note, candidate)

**Recorded:** 2026-11-22
**Provenance:** the value claims below come from Leo Rogers in conversation with Claude (2026-11-22). The structure and formal sketch are Claude's (Claude Opus 5.5).
**Status:** candidate. Claims 1–3 and 5 are **value commitments**, a philosophical position that data cannot prove. Claim 4's formal version and the second-best target are **mathematical definitions** with testable consequences. Nothing here has been tested, and nothing changes [model.md](model.md).

## Why this note exists

ER's long-horizon aim is a framework for digitizing experience. This note sets out the reason experience is the thing worth capturing, and why the framework should not treat a person as a closed system. Two ideas motivate it:

- If not for experience, why should anything matter?
- What makes a life inside one's own imagination insufficient is relationships with real others.

## Five claims

1. **Grounding.** Something matters only if it makes a difference to some subject's experience.
2. **Fit.** An experience can be judged by whether it tracks what is real, not only by how it feels. This is the role of the evidence-relative reference $q(x\mid D)$ in [model.md](model.md).
3. **Other minds.** Other people's experience is as real as one's own, and it matters in its own right, not instrumentally.
4. **Relationship.** A relationship is two real experiences responding to each other. Imagined others cannot supply this, because they are generated from one's own state.
5. **Selection.** The states worth moving toward fit reality, are reachable, and keep one coupled to real others. A device that supplies pleasant experience by giving up claims 2 and 4 fails this test, as in Nozick's experience machine.

## Formal sketch of claim 4: independent coupling

Add another person's affect $e'_t$ to the setting of [model.md](model.md), with own state $s_t$.

- **Imagined other.** $e'_t = f(s_t)$ for some function $f$ of one's own state, possibly with noise generated by one's own process.
- **Real other.** $e'_t$ carries innovations that originate outside one's own process, so it is not a function of $s_t$.

**Mathematical equivalence.** If $e'_t = f(s_t)$, then $I(e'_t;\, e_{t+1} \mid s_t) = 0$. An imagined other adds no information about one's own future beyond what one's own state already holds. This is a direct property of conditioning on a quantity that determines $e'_t$.

**Definition (relational coupling).** A pair is *relationally coupled* when influence runs both ways and each side carries independent information:

$$
I(e'_t;\, e_{t+1} \mid s_t) > 0 \quad\text{and}\quad I(e_t;\, e'_{t+1} \mid s'_t) > 0 .
$$

**Identification caveat.** Observational data alone cannot always separate real coupling from shared context or self-generated variability. Credible tests need interventions on one partner, such as an experimenter-controlled perturbation that the other partner cannot predict from their own state.

**Open question.** Is there a measurable signature of relational coupling in dyadic experience-sampling or lab data that a shared-context model cannot reproduce? This is the natural first test for ER's social-coupling component, which has not been formalized.

## A related candidate: second-best targets

When no fitting state is reachable ($\mathcal E^* \cap \mathcal R_{h,\alpha} = \varnothing$), define the most fitting reachable set

$$
\mathcal E^{**}_{h,\alpha} = \arg\min_{e\in\mathcal R_{h,\alpha}} \bar J_\phi(e\mid D,c),
$$

and the **fit–reach frontier** $m(\alpha) = \min_{e\in\mathcal R_{h,\alpha}} \bar J_\phi(e\mid D,c)$. As $\alpha$ falls, $\mathcal R_{h,\alpha}$ can only grow, so $m(\alpha)$ cannot increase. Asking for less certainty that a state will be reached never worsens the best achievable fit.

**Candidate prediction (not yet registered).** If what people *want* to feel tracks $\mathcal E^{**}$ rather than $\mathcal E^*$, then reported desired emotion (in the sense of Tamir et al., 2017) should shift with manipulated controllability while the evidence stays fixed. In a threat-of-shock design with known contingencies, participants without an escape option should report desiring an intermediate state, not full calm. If desired-emotion reports do not move with controllability, the idea fails.
